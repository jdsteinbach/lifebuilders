## Introduction

When I was a kid I more or less fit the compliant first-born stereotype. The mostly obedient kid who ate up the accolades whenever I succeeded at something. Now, I wasn't a perfect kid. Most of us weren't. One secret disobedience I indulged in through much of my junior high & high school years was ... (please don't judge me when you hear this!) taking my Walkman to bed with me and listening to baseball games on the radio after I was supposed to be asleep. The original headphones had died and I'd gotten a pair of small wired earbuds (younger friends, these were still sort of new-ish and cool in the mid-to-late 90s, we were still a decade away from iPhone EarPods, not even close to wireless anything!). These earbuds conveniently allowed me to appear entirely asleep with just one earbud in. Had my parents checked in on me and found the Walkman, they would've confiscated it and, based on what I recall of confiscations back then, I might never have seen it again. As much as I loved listening to those games, I did so with just a bit of apprehension, a touch of fear that my parents would catch me in the act and I'd lose my Walkman and the joy of baseball.

Sometimes we treat God like a parent in that same way. Jesus might show up when we don't expect him and we'll get caught and so we're kind of nervous and apprehensive and a little afraid. To a large extent, I think we get this as a by-product of our individualistic, introspective culture. We're conditioned to focus on our _self_, a unique person separate from everyone else. On top of that we're conditioned to _focus_ on our self, our gaze turned intently inward. And so thinking about Jesus' return can produce introspective worry and apprehension.

This anxiety induced by our culture is only exacerbated by certain pop Christian ideas and theologies about the Rapture, Tribulation, and Second Coming, etc. The sensationalist theology like what you'd read in the _Left Behind_ books and movies, drawing on the earlier media like the 70s Christian rapture-horror film "A Thief in the Night" and non-fiction books like _The Late Great Planet Earth_, has left an unfortunate scar on the evangelical psyche, conditioning us to be afraid of all that scary apocalyptic stuff around Jesus' return, or even to be low-key afraid of God (I mean, who else is causing those events?)!

Brief disclaimer: I'm not trying to dunk on reasonably argued, non-sensationalist theological positions about what events Christians might expect regarding Jesus' return. There are ways to believe almost any of the orthodox end-times theologies without stirring up fear and unrest. I will observer that certain deeply pessimistic theologies make us pessimistic about Jesus' return.

We affirm every week that "Christ will come again," specifically that "he will come again with glory to judge the living and the dead." Should that make us afraid? Should we live today with the worry I felt as a kid about getting caught listening to late-night baseball?

That's a nuanced question and I'll start by identifying three kinds of fear:
1. Some of us feel a sort of "generalized anxiety" about Jesus' coming back. This could be a specific symptom of diagnosable generalized anxiety disorder, or (as mentioned before) a symptom of an unhelpful theological influence, or some other nebulous source of fear. There may be nothing specific that you feel bad about, just fuzzy apprehension.
2. Maybe something that's wrong in your life jumped right to mind. You may be struggling with a behavior, a habit, or choices that you would be ashamed to get caught in. You're working on it but would still feel bad.
3. Or more seriously, someone may be keeping an intentional grasp on something wrong, not to give it; and that makes you glower more than cower at the idea of Jesus' return.

Real quick sidebar on the 2nd & 3rd options... To be addressing a specific area of brokenness, not perfect, but with God's Spirit and God's people, working on it. There's no shame there. I can assure you, Jesus will not be mad at anyone for being a work in progress. For the third type, that's tougher. While that's not the need this Scripture is addressing, please know that you're among friends. Wrestling with a deep choice like that is hard and there are people in this room who've wrestled like that and people who are open and patient and willing to listen and sometimes talk through hard things. Find one of our clergy or leaders or a wise elder and if you're brave enough to talk, I think you'll be able to find someone here who's happy to listen.

So the focus of this passage and thus this sermon is more for the first category: generic, vague fear or anxiety. I mentioned generalized anxiety disorder: this could be a religious-shaped symptom. This is not a sermon against professional medical care, prescriptions, or any of the other gifts God gives us to be whole and healthy. I do hope these words (from Jesus himself) minister grace and health and flourishing for all of us. Even if anxiety is not part of your experience, these words of Jesus are useful, they are truth given to enable all of us to live rightly in light of Jesus' return.

Here's the punchline and the theme of this message: the return of Jesus shows us the extravagant love of God and we are free to joyfully look forward to it. Jesus makes this clear with two metaphors in this section: (1) the tender love of the Shepherd, (2) the unconventional love of the head of household.

## The Tender Love of the Shepherd

In vs 32, Jesus begins with these tender words: "Do not be afraid, little flock, for it is your Father's good pleasure to give you the kingdom." Jesus has just finished talking about worry and anxiety around earthly needs and he's putting on his rhetorical turn signal now, letting us know he's starting to change lanes. He's shifting to a conversation about his kingdom and especially his return.

Jesus addresses his hearers as "little flock." This title emphasizes vulnerability. A flock of sheep is already a group of vulnerable animals; a little flock, even more so. I think Jesus is also expressing a note of tenderness and affection, addressing his followers with this diminutive label.

What words do vulnerable people need to hear more than "Do not be afraid?" "Fear not" is no empty platitude here; it's not a hollow encouragement with no reason; no toxic positivity. Here's why we should not be afraid...

It's our Father's good pleasure to give us the kingdom. This word "good pleasure" is as nice as it sounds. It means to delight in something, to genuinely enjoy something, to be happily satisfied with something. What makes God so happy, what brings God such joy? Giving us the kingdom. Think about that - God's kingdom. Theologians sometimes struggle to define the kingdom - it's such a big topic. Definitions range, some emphasizing the act of ruling or the experience of God's rule; others lean toward the future and the final placement of all things under Jesus' feet; some try to bring in all of the above. How does Jesus mean it here? In vs 33, the outcome of God's delightful gift that we can sell everything we have and give the proceeds away. The kingdom here is connected directly to provision. The kingdom is more than just stuff, to be sure! A common way to make a big point in Jesus' day was to make a argument from a greater thing to a lesser thing. If this huge thing is true, how much more true is a smaller version? If God is downright delighted to give us the kingdom, how much more can we trust God for all the daily stuff we need? The rest of this metaphor is familiar: we're to store up treasure in heaven, not in the corruptible things of earth. This runs perfectly parallel to what Jesus taught in the Sermon on the Mount as Matthew records it.

Let's roll this all together now and hear these words like Jesus' followers might've heard that day. Addressed as a beloved flock of vulnerable needy sheep, they were told they didn't need any earthly wealth because God is delighted to give them everything they need. I wonder how many of those disciples would've immediately thought, "The Lord is my shepherd; I shall not want." In the face of my need, "he makes me lie down in green pastures, he leads me beside quiet waters." Though I'm small and vulnerable, "I will fear no evil, ... your rod and your staff, they comfort me." God gives us the kingdom, just like "you prepare a table (a banquet) before me, ... you anoint my head with oil," treating me like I'm a king. There's a rich consistency here. Jesus isn't making up something new; he's providing words of deep and real comfort with the same language, the same imagery, the same beauty that God's people have used since David (and before)!

You may be wondering, "hey, that's lovely, but how is that about Jesus' return?" Great question! First, taking the suggestive imagery of royal banquets and anointing oil and making it explicit in the phrase "give you the kingdom" is directing our attention to a future-focus, to expectation. There's a unresolved anticipation in Luke's Gospel (and other) about the kingdom. It's arriving, it's drawing near, it is near, but is it all the way here for real? Not yet! Luke starts writing his volume 2 (Acts) with a story about the disciples asking Jesus is he was going to restore the kingdom to Israel right now. That kingdom that's always "near" - is it "here?" And Jesus answer? "None of your business, guys. The Father has all those dates and times locked in; you just take the Spirit you're about to receive and go change the world." So Jesus' mentioning the kingdom is kind of an intentional lead-in to talking about his return.

What I love here is that before Jesus really gets into that, he soft-launches the topic in a way that's meant to wrap us up in an expression of love that recognizes our vulnerability and fear, hits our awareness of our own need with a glorious promise, and steeps us again in that rich, deep beauty of our ancient connection to our loving Shepherd. All that with a big hint that this is relevant as we look to the future.

## The Unconventional Love of the Head of Household

That brings us to the second big idea in this passage. Verse 35–38 are almost a "micro-parable" about Jesus' return. These few sentences are not big enough to get treated as a full parable. Jesus starts with a couple instructions: be dressed for action, keep the lights on. He then describes household servants awaiting the return of the head of the household who has been away at a wedding feast. This is not a couple hours of festivities like weddings today. One nearby Jewish sources specifies seven days of feasting for a new bride and three for a widow remarrying. Think of John 2 where Jesus saves a wedding feast by creating roughly 150 gallons of _additional_ wine. These celebrations were big deals. Meanwhile back at the ranch, the servants have to be constantly ready for their master's return. They didn't have a written itinerary, a flight number to track, or a Find My Master app on their phones. Waiting and watching at the ready was the only option. Jesus says, "It'll be good for those servants whose master finds them watching when he comes." Some English Bibles say "blessed are those servants" and that's good too. It's the same word repeated in the Beatitudes ("blessed are the poor in heart," "blessed are those servants"). It'll go well, that'd be fortunate, it'll be happy for them. Exactly how fortunate, though?

I love this part. So far we might've been tempted to a little skepticism. Ok, so the whole loving Shepherd bit was lovely and a nice intro to Jesus' teaching about his return, but that wasn't just a bait and switch, was it? Is it "it's a good thing about that loving Shepherd and the kingdom, because yikes, here comes the big, scary return of Jesus!" Nothing could be further from the truth!

Here's how blessed those servants will be. Imagine how much energy those week-long weddings would cost you. Introverted friends, I'm sorry, you're excused from this part of the assignment: you don't have to imagine that if you don't want to. Now after an event like that, you're probably going to walk home. Depending on what part of Palestine you lived in then, closer villages in populated areas were a few miles away (an hour or so walk) while less dense areas could require 2-3 hours of walking to get to the nearest village. Maybe this head of household is at a wedding in his own town and only needs 5-10 minutes to get home; maybe it's a farther village and he's got a day's journey or more. Jesus doesn't specify; that only adds to the mystery of his return timing for the audience. Now put yourself in his shoes: you've just been at a long, potentially exhausting family celebration for days. You've walked home. How are you feeling? I'm feeling like it's unwind time. If I had enough energy, I might empty my suitcase into the laundry hampers or at least try to get it near the laundry room. I want my kids to put themselves to bed. I want some comfy clothes, a drink, a snack, and a spot on the couch. I want time to unwind with a show maybe. Look at the middle of vs 37 though! "Truly I tell you, he will dress himself to serve, and will have them recline at the table and will come and wait on them." The servants have stayed dressed to serve; their lord gets dressed to serve them. They've waited for him; now he waits on them.

How extravagant this is! This is a complete and utter upheaval of expectations. Jesus is not only master of the wind and waves, he's also the master of the surprise ending and the plot twist! The extravagant, unexpected, upside-down love of God is on full display right here, right in the very story Jesus uses to call his followers to vigilance and readiness while we await his return. How's that feel in the face of anxiety and trepidation? We may feel apprehensive, but Jesus' own words set us up with the expectation: when we watch and wait for him, he returns to exalt us!

The extravagance of the Lord's love shines even more brightly if we turn a few pages in Luke's Gospel and read 17.7–10:

> Who among you would say to your slave who has just come in from plowing or tending sheep in the field, "Come here at once and take your place at the table"? *8* Would you not rather say to him, "Prepare supper for me, put on your apron and serve me while I eat and drink; later you may eat and drink"? *9* Do you thank the slave for doing what was commanded? *10* So you also, when you have done all that you were ordered to do, say, "We are worthless slaves; we have done only what we ought to have done!"

Jesus isn't contradicting himself here; Luke isn't a messy author or sloppy editor. Jesus is using the same imagery (God as lord; us as servants) to make two different points. In Luke 17, his story follows cultural expectations for lords and servants to commend humility and to warn against self-aggrandizement. In Luke 12, his story subverts cultural realities to surprise us with the spiritual reality of God's ridiculously unconventional love for us.

Quick aside: finding these opposing uses of lord/servant imagery (one conventional, one subversive) within a few chapters of each other reminds me of the danger of grabbing one true thing and treating it like it's the only true thing. If you grabbed onto Luke 17 and treated "we are worthless slaves" like it was the whole truth about your servant-to-lord relationship with God, how hurtful and limiting spiritually constraining that would that be. I know this, because for a time, back in college, I did just that. I treated Luke 17.10 like some kind of catchphrase, thinking that kind of lopsided self-deprecation was a Christian virtue. That pseudo-spiritual pessimism will stunt our flourishing in many ways, including this - it sure doesn't make it easy to look forward to Jesus' return. Why would a "worthless slave" want his lord to come back? Even though that piece of the truth about humility was helpful in exposing patterns of selfishness and arrogance I needed to face at the time, when I escalated it to near-mantra status, it brought some damaging side-effects. Feeling the tension between both metaphors, both Luke 12 and Luke 17, would've been helpful back then.

So while on one hand, we may wrestle with some apprehension about Jesus' return and the realization that we're not meant to exalt ourselves at all (Lk 17) could stir that up, Luke 12 blows that out of the water. We don't even need to exalt ourselves, to think more highly of ourselves than we should because no one exalts us, no one thinks more highly of us than our Lord. That's what the actions of the head of household prove - our Lord is eager to grab a servant's robe, seat us around the table, and serve up a feast. Our shepherd happily prepares a banquet for us sheep and royally anoints us. It delights our loving God to give us the kingdom!

Are you struggling to feel eager about the "return of Christ?" Are you unsure what to expect or worried about what that return might bring? Here's the relief Jesus offers: rest in the character of the one who is returning. Meditate on the love, the delight, the joy, the extravagance of our returning Lord and rest in that.
